# Draft Results Section — Workplace-Focused Systematic Review

## Study selection and characteristics

The updated workplace-focused search identified an active evidence pool of 34 primary studies published between 2015 and 2026. The studies included randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, analytical cross-sectional studies, and quasi-experimental workplace interventions. Occupational populations included office and white-collar workers, police and security personnel, commercial and tanker drivers, manufacturing and petrochemical workers, healthcare professionals, university staff, military personnel, heavy-industry workers, and employees enrolled in workplace health-promotion programmes.

The evidence base was methodologically heterogeneous. Randomized and quasi-experimental studies primarily evaluated workplace exercise, dietary, or multicomponent lifestyle interventions, whereas observational studies examined occupational stress, physical activity, sedentary behaviour, diet quality, occupational category, and related lifestyle factors in relation to metabolic syndrome (MetS) or established MetS components.

Preliminary JBI appraisal indicated generally stronger methodological structure among the randomized and prospective cohort studies than among cross-sectional and uncontrolled intervention studies. The principal limitations across the evidence base were cross-sectional temporality, incomplete reporting of allocation concealment and attrition procedures in some trials, and absence of concurrent randomized control groups in several workplace intervention studies.

## Occupational and work-related stress

Prospective evidence supported an association between adverse work stress and subsequent MetS risk. In a five-year cohort of police officers, Garbarino and Magnavita reported that high work stress was associated with increased odds of incident MetS (adjusted OR 2.68, 95% CI 1.08–6.70). Similarly, in the Furukawa Nutrition and Health Study, workers whose job demands increased from low to high over three years had higher odds of developing MetS than workers with persistently low job demands (adjusted OR 3.27, 95% CI 1.46–7.34).

Cross-sectional studies generally pointed in the same direction, although they cannot establish temporality. Among security guards, higher occupational stressors were associated with adverse fasting glucose, triglyceride, cholesterol, blood-pressure, and cardiovascular-risk profiles after covariate adjustment. In petrochemical workers, occupational stress measured using job-content and effort–reward imbalance frameworks was associated with MetS and selected metabolic components. Among Ghanaian military personnel, work stress was associated with higher odds of MetS (OR 6.47, 95% CI 1.54–27.29), although the wide confidence interval indicates considerable uncertainty in the magnitude of the association.

Not all studies showed a consistent stress effect. In some Ghanaian university and healthcare-worker samples, dietary factors were more clearly associated with metabolic markers than work stress. Taken together, the evidence suggests that chronic or increasing occupational stress may contribute to MetS risk, with the strongest support coming from prospective worker cohorts.

## Diet and workplace nutrition

Dietary evidence included both observational studies and workplace interventions. In office workers, low fruit intake was associated with MetS alongside low leisure-time physical activity. Among Iranian health workers, a diet-moderation score was associated with MetS (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.008–1.05) and with abdominal obesity, elevated triglycerides, and elevated fasting blood glucose, whereas the dietary-adequacy score showed weaker or null associations.

Among Ghanaian tanker drivers, MetS prevalence was 19.6%, and dietary quality and energy adequacy were among the lifestyle characteristics assessed alongside clinical predictors. Among workers at a multinational company in Madrid, greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with lower odds of MetS (OR 0.45, 95% CI 0.31–0.68), suggesting a potentially protective dietary pattern in occupational settings.

Intervention evidence was also supportive. A randomized canteen-based dietary intervention among occupational men improved several MetS components, including fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, waist circumference, and body mass index. Earlier nonrandomized worksite dietary programmes also reported improvement in MetS indicators following nutrition education, counselling, and modification of the workplace food environment.

Overall, the dietary literature suggests that both habitual dietary quality and structured workplace nutrition programmes are associated with meaningful differences in MetS risk and component profiles.

## Physical activity and sedentary behaviour

Physical activity showed one of the most consistent associations with metabolic health across observational and intervention studies. In a prospective cohort of 22,383 workers, higher levels of mixed moderate and vigorous leisure-time exercise were associated with lower MetS incidence, with hazard ratios of 0.74 (95% CI 0.62–0.89) for 7.5 to <16.5 MET-hours/week and 0.81 (95% CI 0.69–0.96) for higher activity. In contrast, commuting walking was not clearly associated with MetS incidence.

Among sedentary occupation workers, those meeting physical-activity recommendations had lower odds of MetS (OR 0.52, 95% CI 0.27–0.98), abdominal obesity (OR 0.36, 95% CI 0.16–0.82), elevated blood pressure (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.26–0.84), and low HDL cholesterol (OR 0.54, 95% CI 0.31–0.93). In Taiwanese workers, high leisure-time physical activity was likewise associated with lower odds of MetS (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.62–0.95), while commuting physical activity showed less consistent associations.

Sedentary behaviour was independently relevant in some populations. Among working adults in Ethiopia, sedentary time of at least eight hours per day was associated with substantially higher MetS prevalence (adjusted prevalence ratio 2.29, 95% CI 1.88–2.80).

Randomized intervention studies reinforced the observational findings. A telemonitoring-supported exercise programme among company employees reduced MetS severity, with a between-group difference of −0.26 (95% CI −0.35 to −0.16). Workplace T'ai Chi, structured physical-activity programmes, and remote activity interventions also improved selected metabolic markers and physiological outcomes. Collectively, these studies support leisure-time and structured workplace physical activity as potentially modifiable determinants of MetS risk.

## Multicomponent workplace lifestyle interventions

Several studies evaluated interventions combining physical activity, dietary modification, counselling, self-monitoring, or digital support. A voluntary worksite weight-loss programme involving 3,880 employees from 93 companies reduced MetS prevalence from 43% to 30% in women and from 52% to 26% in men over the intervention period.

In Korean office workers, a targeted workplace health-promotion programme improved waist circumference and fasting glucose and reduced MetS prevalence from 41.5% to 31.7% in the intensive intervention group. Internet-based lifestyle counselling among Korean workers improved weight, visceral fat, and waist circumference, while a web-based high-intensity interval-training plus calorie-restriction programme improved body weight, blood pressure, and triglycerides, with several benefits maintained at one year.

The strongest recent evidence came from randomized programmes. A cluster-randomized healthy-lifestyle education intervention among Ethiopian bank employees reduced waist circumference by approximately 5.3 cm, systolic blood pressure by 7.0 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure by 4.2 mmHg, total cholesterol by 34.1 mg/dL, and LDL cholesterol by 20.7 mg/dL. These findings indicate that workplace-based multicomponent programmes can produce clinically relevant improvements in several MetS risk factors.

## Summary of evidence consistency

Across designs, the evidence generally converged on three patterns. First, adverse occupational stress was associated with greater MetS risk, with prospective studies providing the strongest temporal support. Second, healthier dietary patterns and workplace dietary interventions were associated with more favorable metabolic profiles. Third, greater physical activity and lower sedentary exposure were consistently associated with reduced MetS risk, and workplace exercise interventions improved several metabolic outcomes.

However, the strength of inference varied substantially across studies. Cross-sectional associations should not be interpreted causally, several quasi-experimental interventions lacked concurrent control groups, and the review includes heterogeneous worker populations, exposure definitions, and MetS criteria. These limitations support a narrative rather than pooled synthesis unless sufficient methodological comparability can be established for specific outcome subsets.
