# Draft Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a clustering of cardiometabolic abnormalities that commonly includes central obesity, elevated blood pressure, impaired glucose regulation, hypertriglyceridaemia, and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Its clinical importance lies in the substantially increased risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and premature morbidity associated with the accumulation of these abnormalities.

For working adults, MetS is especially relevant because many of its determinants are shaped by the conditions in which people work. Sedentary occupations can reduce daily energy expenditure, irregular or extended working hours may disrupt eating patterns and recovery, and high psychosocial demands may reinforce behaviours such as physical inactivity and unhealthy food choices. Occupational stress may also influence metabolic risk through neuroendocrine pathways and prolonged physiological activation. These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive: work stress, diet, physical activity, and sedentary behaviour may interact over time rather than operate as isolated exposures.

Evidence from occupational populations supports this concern. Prospective studies of police officers and manufacturing workers have linked higher or increasing work stress with subsequent MetS risk, while large workforce cohorts have demonstrated meaningful differences in MetS incidence across occupational groups. Physical activity also appears relevant, with prospective worker studies suggesting lower MetS incidence among those engaging in greater leisure-time exercise, whereas prolonged sedentary exposure has been associated with higher MetS prevalence. Workplace dietary patterns and nutrition interventions provide a further modifiable pathway, with observational and intervention studies reporting associations between diet quality, abdominal obesity, glycaemic markers, lipid profiles, and MetS.

Workplaces therefore represent both a source of metabolic risk and a practical setting for prevention. Interventions delivered through worksites can potentially reach adults who may not otherwise participate in structured prevention programmes. Randomized and quasi-experimental studies have evaluated exercise, dietary modification, digital health support, counselling, self-monitoring, and multicomponent lifestyle programmes, with several reporting improvements in MetS severity or individual components.

Despite this growing literature, the evidence remains fragmented. Studies differ substantially in occupational populations, exposure definitions, intervention intensity, MetS criteria, outcome reporting, and methodological design. Some reviews of MetS have focused on general adult populations or clinical samples rather than employed populations, while workplace-health reviews have often examined single behaviours or broad cardiovascular outcomes without synthesizing occupational stress, diet, physical activity, and MetS together.

A focused synthesis of workplace evidence is therefore needed to clarify which occupational and behavioural factors are most consistently associated with MetS, which findings are supported by prospective or intervention evidence, and where important methodological gaps remain.

The aim of this systematic review was to synthesize evidence on the relationships between workplace stress, dietary factors, physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and MetS among working adults. A secondary aim was to evaluate the effectiveness of workplace-based lifestyle interventions targeting MetS or its established metabolic components.
